Step 1 — your usage

Upload your own half-hourly usage

Works with a half-hourly (or hourly) usage export from any supplier or smart-meter app — see the format guide below for what's needed. Everything below then recalculates against your real usage pattern instead of the example.

What the file needs
  • A CSV with one row per meter reading, containing at minimum: a consumption/usage column in kWh and a reading start-time column.
  • Column headers are matched flexibly — anything containing "consumption", "kWh" or "usage" for the reading, and "start", "date" or "time" for the timestamp works. Extra columns (cost, standing charge, end time, etc.) are ignored.
  • Timestamps should be in YYYY-MM-DDTHH:MM format (ISO 8601), e.g. 2026-01-15T14:30:00+00:00 — this is the standard format almost every UK supplier and smart-meter app exports.
  • Half-hourly readings give the most accurate result; hourly works too but timing detail is coarser.
Where to get it
  • Most UK suppliers have a usage/consumption CSV export in your online account or app.
  • If yours doesn't, free tools like n3rgy, Loop or Hildebrand Glow can pull half-hourly data straight from your smart meter, regardless of supplier.
  • The more history you upload — ideally a full year — the more the seasonal estimate reflects reality.

Other things worth knowing about battery sizing

  • Usable vs. nameplate capacity. The headline kWh figure on a spec sheet is sometimes the gross/nameplate capacity rather than what you can actually draw on — ask installers specifically for the usable capacity after depth-of-discharge limits, since that's what this tool assumes throughout.
  • Power rating matters as much as capacity. A battery's kW output (not just its kWh capacity) determines whether it can cover a spike in demand — an oven, a kettle and an EV charger running together can exceed what a small inverter can deliver, even with plenty of charge still stored.
  • Typical UK household sizes. Most homes land around 5–10 kWh usable; households with an EV, a heat pump, or a larger family often go for 10–20 kWh, sometimes as stacked/modular units from the same manufacturer.
  • Diminishing returns are real. Sizing for a typical day rather than your single heaviest day is usually the better financial call — the last few kWh of a "cover every worst case" battery often sit unused for most of the year, as the chart above shows for your own usage.
  • Chemistry. Most modern home batteries use LFP (lithium iron phosphate) cells — more thermally stable than older lithium chemistries, typically rated for 6,000+ cycles and backed by 10-year warranties.
  • Degradation. Usable capacity fades a little each year — commonly a few percent per year over a decade — so a bit of headroom above your calculated minimum helps the system still meet your needs later in its warranty life.
  • Expandable systems. Many batteries are modular — it's often cheaper to start slightly smaller and add a second unit later if you need it than to oversize from day one and pay for capacity you rarely draw on.

What "specific yield" means
  • It's the energy a solar array produces per year for every kWp of panels installed — in kWh/kWp/yr. Multiply it by your array size (shown below as "Array size") to get expected annual generation.
  • It depends on your location, roof pitch, compass orientation, and any shading — not just panel quality — so it varies a fair amount between two otherwise-identical systems on different roofs.
  • Across the UK it typically runs roughly 850–1,050 kWh/kWp/yr for an unshaded, south-facing roof at a good pitch — lower in the north, higher in the south, and lower still on east/west-facing or shaded roofs. The 970 default here is a reasonable mid-UK, south-facing estimate — not your figure.
How to get your own figure
  • Free and most accurate DIY option: the EU's PVGIS tool (photovoltaic-software.com or re.jrc.ec.europa.eu/pvg_tools) — enter your postcode/coordinates plus your roof's tilt and compass direction, and it returns a site-specific yield estimate.
  • Simplest option: ask your installer for the annual generation (kWh) figure from their MCS-compliant design software — divide it by your array's kWp to get the specific yield they've assumed, or just enter their kWh figure directly by adjusting this field until "Modelled generation" above matches theirs.
  • After installation, your MCS certificate will show the yield estimate actually used for your install — worth checking that against what you assumed here.
